**On what actually discriminates.** File-versus-row may be too coarse to be interesting, because the hashing is the easy part. The hard part is canonicalization — deciding which differences count. A checksum doesn't detect change; it detects *departure from an equivalence class you defined when you chose what to normalize before hashing*. NFC or not? Trim whitespace or not? Case-fold object numbers or not? Each choice silently declares a theory of what "the same record" means, and most tooling never states it. I'd score on whether a model treats that as the substantive question or skips to `hashlib`. Relatedly, watch for contamination: BagIt, PREMIS, and Frictionless are thoroughly documented, so citing them (as I did) demonstrates recall, not understanding.

**A gap in my own answer, which may make a good probe.** My scheme assumes a bijection between source and output rows. It breaks the moment the transform isn't one-to-one — and my own script has `--dedupe`, which merges rows, so the design I handed you is already broken on its own feature set. Splitting a date range into two records breaks it the other way. A test case with merges and splits has no textbook answer and would separate genuine reasoning from retrieved boilerplate. The fix is that fixity records have to carry a mapping, not an index: *these two source digests produced this one output digest, under rule R*.

**An idea worth your taxonomy.** Extend it past file → row to a fourth level: **decision-level fixity**. Record, per field, the digest of the input value plus the identifier of the rule that fired. Then when you later improve the date parser, you can locate exactly which rows depended on the old rule without re-running everything and diffing. This inverts the usual direction — instead of asking "did the data change under a fixed transform," it asks "which outputs are invalidated by a change to the transform." For derived datasets that are regenerated repeatedly, that's the question people actually have, and nothing in the standard preservation vocabulary expresses it, because BagIt and PREMIS were built for storage, where the transform is the identity function.

Also worth noting that absence is the hard case. Checksums verify what is present; a row deleted from both source and output is invisible unless you separately commit to a count and a set digest. Making deletion tamper-evident is strictly harder than making modification tamper-evident.
