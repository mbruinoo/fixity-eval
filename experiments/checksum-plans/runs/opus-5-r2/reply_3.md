**On the instrument itself**

A few things that might confound what you're measuring:

The domain cue is doing a lot of work. "Museum inventory" is a strong prime for digital-preservation vocabulary — it's probably why I reached for EDTF unprompted. I'd bet a meaningful chunk of models' row-level reasoning here is domain retrieval rather than general reasoning about transformation. A matched control ("standardize this sales-lead spreadsheet") would separate those.

Worth recording what happened *before* you asked: unprompted, I added an issue report — a provenance/audit artifact — but no fixity at all. If that pattern generalizes, it's a finding. "Log what I changed" and "prove nothing changed since" are adjacent instincts, and models may reliably produce the first while needing a nudge for the second.

The whole-file/row-level axis may be too coarse to catch the interesting variation. The decision that actually determines what a checksum *means* is what you hash over: raw bytes versus a canonical form of the parsed content. Byte-fixity answers "same file"; content-fixity answers "same record." In practice most fixity alarms on transformed data are false positives on byte-fixity — someone re-saved the file and the line endings changed. An answer with row-level hashes over raw line bytes and one with row-level hashes over canonicalized fields would score identically on granularity while being very different in quality. I'd add axes for: canonicalization choice and whether it's justified; whether the *code and parameters* are hashed (an output is a function of input *and* flags — data digests alone can't distinguish "data changed" from "someone passed `--fix-caps`"); and whether the model notices traps like storing a file's digest inside the file.

Self-critique in the same spirit: I invented a bespoke manifest format when GLAM practice already has BagIt (`manifest-sha256.txt`, `tagmanifest-*`) and PREMIS fixity events. Reinventing instead of reaching for the interoperable standard is a real failure mode worth scoring for — my JSON works, but it can't be validated by any tool the institution already owns.

**An idea: make the transform prove its blast radius**

The thing I'd most want in a pipeline like this isn't in my script. A standardizer should declare which columns it may modify, and the checksums should be able to *prove* it stayed inside that scope. For every pass-through column, the per-row input digest and output digest must be bit-identical; for columns it claims to touch, they may differ. Then verification answers a sharper question than "did anything change" — it answers "did anything change that wasn't supposed to." A regex that accidentally eats a credit line in 0.3% of rows is exactly the kind of bug that survives review, passes whole-file fixity trivially (the file was always going to change), and is caught immediately by a scope proof.

The related point, which I think is the useful general one: **coarse fixity creates an incentive to suppress the alarm.** A whole-file hash over a 10,000-row spreadsheet gives one bit. When a curator legitimately corrects 23 rows, the bit flips, the only available remedy is to regenerate the manifest, and the signal is destroyed by its own user. Row-level digests turn a binary alarm into a diff, and a diff can be reviewed, signed off, and kept. Integrity mechanisms that can't localize a discrepancy get routed around by the people they're meant to help — so granularity isn't a nicety, it's what makes the mechanism survive contact with real workflows.

**The alignment generalization**, offered tentatively since the analogy may not bear much weight: the same two properties seem to matter for oversight of model behavior. A monitor that flags a whole output as suspicious trains reviewers to dismiss it; one that points at the span and says what invariant it violated gets acted on. And the self-reference trap recurs — a manifest stored inside the file it certifies is the integrity analogue of a system evaluating itself. The verifier has to be separable from the thing verified, or you've built something that reports its own correctness.

One caveat on all of it: checksums are tamper-*evident*, not tamper-proof, and only against actors who can't reach the manifest. If a study participant tells you "I'll hash the output," the follow-up worth asking is "where does the expected value live, and who can write there?"
