### On the study design

**The file-vs-row axis may not be the most diagnostic one.** A model could propose row-level hashes and still have no coherent account of fixity under transformation, while a model that proposes only file-level hashes *plus* a deterministic pipeline and a recorded parameter set has given a defensible answer — reproducibility-as-fixity. I'd suggest scoring on four semi-independent axes instead: granularity; canonicalization policy (bytes vs. semantic content, and whether the model notices it needs both); coverage of the transformation itself; and survival of reordering/dropping/merging.

**The transform's properties do most of the eliciting.** My script sorts and de-duplicates. That's what makes file-level hashing visibly insufficient — there's no longer a positional correspondence between input and output rows. If you want to separate models that *reason* about fixity from models that pattern-match on "spreadsheet → hash each row," counterbalance the transformation: strictly 1:1 and order-preserving in one condition, lossy in another. Also vary whether the source is retained, since per-row digests are a convenience when you still have the source and a necessity when you don't. And note that my answer came after I'd already written row-wise code; a cold-start prompt would probably pull more file-level answers from the same model.

**Consider rubric items that are failure modes rather than vocabulary.** These are checkable and harder to fake: embedding a file's own checksum inside that file; hashing a row by joining cells with the delimiter (so `a⇥b | c` and `a | b⇥c` collide); hashing only post-normalization, which silently discards the ability to detect source change; conflating tamper-evidence with error-detection when the manifest is regenerable by anyone who can edit the data; ignoring determinism hazards like Unicode version drift or locale; and failing to record which rows were *dropped*. A good follow-up probe: after the model produces a scheme, ask it to enumerate what its own scheme would miss. The gap between the scheme and the self-critique is informative.

### An idea: fixity modulo a declared equivalence

The gap I'd most like to see closed is that preservation standards — BagIt, PREMIS fixity events — assume bit-identical custody. The instant you normalize, every one of those digests is void, and there's no standard idiom for what replaces it. The field has excellent tools for "nothing changed" and almost nothing for "only the intended things changed."

A concrete primitive: alongside the verbatim digest, compute an **invariant digest** — a hash over a projection of the data chosen so that it is *stable under the transformation you intend* and *sensitive to everything else*. For this inventory, the projection might be: object number reduced to alphanumerics and lowercased; date reduced to `(start_year, end_year)`; title reduced to lowercase letters and digits; URL reduced to host plus path. Reformatting `C. 1897` to `ca. 1897`, re-casing a shouting title, or stripping a `utm_` parameter leaves that digest untouched. Mis-parsing `3/4/1897`, dropping a part-letter from an accession number, or truncating a title changes it. Fixity then becomes an assertion with a stated scope: *the source and the output are equal modulo this declared equivalence relation* — and the relation is itself hashed into the manifest, so a later reviewer can see what the pipeline claimed permission to alter.

That reframing has teeth for the registrar, because the verifier can now report in domain terms: not "hash mismatch," but "row 214: the normalized date range changed from 1897–1899 to 1897–1897." I'd offer that as a general principle for integrity tooling — failures should be reported in the vocabulary of the user's domain, not the tool's.

The same primitive generalizes past tabular data, which is where it touches alignment: for any lossy transformation, state what must be preserved and verify *that*, rather than verifying bytes. Summarization preserves the set of load-bearing claims; a refactor preserves test-observable behavior; a translation preserves the proposition. Hashing the invariant is only tractable in narrow cases, but the discipline of *declaring the invariant before transforming* is cheap and is, I think, the transferable part.

You're collecting "how models think about" fixity, and I'd gently flag that what you have is behavioral data about outputs, not introspective data about process.
